Tara Unconquerable, Full of Splendor
སྒྲོལ་མ་མི་ཕམ་གཟི་བརྗིད་ཅན།

Nineteenth among the twenty-one, she is attended by the king of the gods — variously named as Indra or Brahma across different commentaries — together with the kinnaras, the celestial musicians of Indian myth, all drawn to serve her not out of obligation but out of sheer reverence for what she radiates. Her own name announces her as invincible, and full of a splendor so complete it becomes her actual method of protection.

She is shown in white, with a single face and two arms, her manner calm rather than aggressive — and this points to something distinctive about how she works. Rather than meeting conflict with counter-force, she dispels disputes and dispels bad dreams simply through the brilliance of her own presence, the way a single steady light entering a confused, dark room can end an argument no words could settle, or the way a nightmare loses its grip the instant a lamp is lit beside the bed. Two traditions describe her form somewhat differently — one centered on a vase of fire blazing at her heart, another on a white parasol edged with sparks of vajra light — but both preserve this same quality of radiant, unforced authority.

To call upon her is to ask for a splendor that resolves conflict not by defeating an opponent but by making the confusion that fueled it simply impossible to sustain in her presence.
